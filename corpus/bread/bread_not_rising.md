# Why Didn't My Bread Rise? Six Common Causes

Source: https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2014/08/04/my-bread-didnt-rise
Author: PJ Hamel (King Arthur Baking)
Published: 2014-08-04
Topic: Yeast bread troubleshooting

## Overview

Bread that looks short or dense does not always have a yeast problem. The article identifies six reasons a yeast loaf may not rise as tall as expected: the pan is too large, the dough rises and collapses, the dough is too dry, whole-grain flour limits expansion, the exposed dough surface dries out, or the dough contains too much sugar.

## 1. The loaf pan is too large

**Symptom:** The bread looks low even though the dough expanded.

**Cause:** A larger pan lets the dough spread outward rather than upward. A 9 x 5-inch loaf pan has roughly 30% more capacity than an 8 1/2 x 4 1/2-inch pan, so the same dough can produce a noticeably shorter loaf.

**What to do:** Follow the pan dimensions specified in the recipe. As a rough guide from the source, a loaf made with about 3 cups of flour often suits an 8 1/2 x 4 1/2-inch pan; around 4 cups often suits a 9 x 5-inch pan. Between those quantities, either may work, with different loaf heights and shapes.

## 2. The bread rose, then collapsed

**Symptom:** Dough initially rises but loses volume before or during baking.

**Cause:** Allowing a shaped loaf to rise too far can leave it unable to retain its structure. Maximum height during proofing is not necessarily the correct point to bake.

**What to do:** Avoid treating the highest possible rise as the target. Watch the loaf's readiness to bake, using proofing guidance appropriate to the recipe. The source points readers to a separate guide for more detailed readiness tests; it does not provide those tests in this article.

## 3. The dough is too dry

**Symptom:** The dough breaks into separate pieces during kneading, and the finished loaf is heavy, dense, or rises unevenly.

**Cause:** Too much flour or too little water creates a stiff dough. Moisture supports yeast activity and helps the gluten network stretch as gas develops.

**What to do:** Measure ingredients carefully, ideally by weight. Avoid repeatedly adding flour while kneading; a lightly greased work surface can reduce sticking without drying the dough. Properly hydrated dough should be soft and somewhat tacky rather than hard or crumbly.

**Source example:** Two otherwise identical loaves differed by only 2 tablespoons of water, yet the drier one rose less evenly.

## 4. High whole-grain content limits rising

**Symptom:** Whole-wheat or mixed-grain bread rises less than a similar white-flour loaf.

**Cause:** Replacing refined wheat flour with whole-wheat flour or adding rye, oats, or bran changes how effectively the dough forms and uses an extensible gluten network.

**What to do:** Expect different rise characteristics when changing the flour. For a 100% whole-wheat loaf, use a recipe designed for whole-wheat flour rather than assuming a white-bread recipe will behave identically after substitution.

## 5. The surface dries during proofing

**Symptom:** The top forms a dry, firm skin and the loaf struggles to expand.

**Cause:** A hardened surface resists stretching. A dish towel keeps dust off the dough but may not retain enough moisture.

**What to do:** Cover the pan in a way that holds in moisture without sticking to the dough. The source suggests a loose clear plastic shower cap over the loaf pan. If using plastic wrap, take care that it does not adhere to the rising dough and cause deflation when removed.

## 6. There is too much sugar

**Symptom:** Sweet enriched dough rises very slowly or not at all.

**Cause:** Sugar binds available water, making it harder for ordinary yeast to function. The source notes that sugar weighing about 10% or more of the flour weight can slow rising substantially.

**What to do:** Check the amount of sugar relative to the flour by weight. For doughs intentionally high in sugar, consider an osmotolerant yeast intended for sweet doughs.

**Source example:** A dough with 12 3/4 oz flour and 1 3/4 oz sugar has sugar equal to about 14% of the flour weight.

## Quick troubleshooting checklist

- Short, wide loaf? Check the pan size first.
- Loaf rose and then sank? Review proofing time and readiness.
- Dough is stiff or breaks apart? Recheck hydration and flour measurements.
- Added whole grains? Use a suitable formula and adjust expectations.
- Dry skin on the surface? Keep the dough covered while rising.
- Very sweet dough? Consider sugar's effect on yeast and the yeast type used.

## Scope of this source

This article focuses on these six causes. It does not provide a comprehensive diagnostic procedure for expired yeast, water temperature, or all fermentation failures. Use additional sources for those questions.
